## Unit 1: One-Variable Data (Exploring Distributions)

Unit 1 lays the bedrock for the entire course: describing distributions, calculating summary statistics, and identifying potential anomalies.

### 1. Describing Quantitative Distributions: The SOCS Framework
When an AP exam free-response question (FRQ) asks you to "describe" or "compare" distributions, you must address all four components **in context**:
- **S — Shape**: Is the distribution symmetric, skewed right (tail stretches toward higher numbers), skewed left (tail stretches toward lower numbers), uniform, or bimodal?
- **O — Outliers**: Are there any unusually distant values? State their approximate values or calculate them formally.
- **C — Center**: State the median (resistant) for skewed data, or the arithmetic mean for symmetric data.
- **S — Spread**: State the interquartile range (`IQR = Q3 - Q1`) for skewed data, or standard deviation (`s`) for symmetric data.

### 2. Five-Number Summary & Quartiles
- **Five-Number Summary**: `Minimum, Q1 (25th percentile), Median (50th percentile), Q3 (75th percentile), Maximum`
- **Interquartile Range**: `IQR = Q3 - Q1` (the middle 50% of the data)

### 3. Tukey's 1.5×IQR Outlier Fences
- **Lower Inner Fence**: `Lower Fence = Q1 - (1.5 × IQR)`
- **Upper Inner Fence**: `Upper Fence = Q3 + (1.5 × IQR)`
- Any observation falling strictly below the Lower Fence (`x < Lower Fence`) or strictly above the Upper Fence (`x > Upper Fence`) is flagged as an outlier. Read our complete [outlier detection guide](/blog/outlier-dot-plot-maker/) for step-by-step worked examples.

### 4. Mean vs. Median & Skewness
- **Symmetric Distribution**: `Mean ≈ Median`
- **Right-Skewed Distribution**: `Mean > Median` (the mean is pulled toward the long right tail)
- **Left-Skewed Distribution**: `Mean < Median` (the mean is pulled toward the long left tail)
- **Standard Deviation (`s`)**: Measures typical distance of data points from the mean. Like the mean, it is non-resistant and sensitive to outliers.

## Unit 2: Two-Variable Data & Linear Regression

Unit 2 explores bivariate quantitative relationships, scatterplots, correlation, and least-squares regression lines (LSRL).

### 1. Correlation Coefficient (`r`)
- Measures the **strength and direction** of a linear relationship between two quantitative variables.
- Bound between `-1` and `+1` (`-1 ≤ r ≤ 1`).
- `r > 0` indicates a positive linear association; `r < 0` indicates a negative linear association.
- `r = 0` indicates no linear association (a strong non-linear curved relationship can still have `r ≈ 0`).
- Switching x and y does not change `r`. Changing units of measurement does not change `r`.

### 2. Least-Squares Regression Line (LSRL)
The line that minimizes the sum of squared vertical residuals:
`y_hat = a + bx`

Where:
- `y_hat` is the **predicted value** of the response variable (always write the hat symbol on the AP exam!).
- `b` is the **slope**: `b = r × (sy / sx)`
  *(Slope Interpretation: For each additional 1-unit increase in x, the predicted y changes by b units in context).*
- `a` is the **y-intercept**: `a = y_bar - b × x_bar`
  *(Intercept Interpretation: When x is 0 units, the predicted y is a units in context).*

### 3. Residuals & Residual Plots
- **Residual Formula**:
  `Residual = Actual - Predicted = y - y_hat`
  *(Mnemonic: "AP" — Actual minus Predicted).*
- **Residual Plot Interpretation**: A linear model is appropriate if the residual plot shows a **random scatter of points** with no discernible curve or fan-shaped pattern.

### 4. Coefficient of Determination (`r²`)
- `r²` represents the **proportion of variation in the response variable (y) that is explained by the linear relationship with the explanatory variable (x)**.
- Example: If `r = 0.8`, then `r² = 0.64`. "64% of the variation in car prices is explained by the linear model with mileage."

## Unit 3: Collecting Data (Sampling & Experiments)

Unit 3 tests your understanding of data collection integrity. On FRQs, precision in statistical vocabulary is strictly graded.

### 1. Sampling Methods
- **Simple Random Sample (SRS)**: Every possible group of size `n` has an equal probability of being selected.
- **Stratified Random Sample**: Population is divided into homogeneous groups (strata) based on a characteristic (e.g., grade level), and an SRS is chosen from *each* stratum. Guarantees representation across subgroups.
- **Cluster Sample**: Population is divided into heterogeneous, naturally occurring groups (clusters) that each resemble the population (e.g., homerooms). Entire clusters are randomly selected, and *all* individuals in chosen clusters are measured.
- **Systematic Sample**: Select every `k`-th individual after a random starting point.

### 2. Observational Studies vs. Experiments
- **Observational Study**: Researchers observe without imposing treatments. Can establish **association/correlation**, but **never causation** due to potential confounding variables.
- **Experiment**: Researchers deliberately impose treatments on experimental units. A well-designed randomized experiment is the **only method that can establish cause-and-effect**.

### 3. The Four Principles of Experimental Design
1. **Comparison**: Use two or more treatments (including a control group) to prevent confounding.
2. **Random Assignment**: Randomly assign subjects to treatments to balance out unmeasured confounding variables across groups.
3. **Control**: Keep extraneous variables constant between treatment groups.
4. **Replication**: Use enough experimental units in each treatment group to distinguish genuine effects from chance variation.

### 4. Common Biases
- **Undercoverage**: Some groups of the population are left out of the sampling frame.
- **Nonresponse Bias**: Selected individuals cannot be contacted or refuse to participate.
- **Voluntary Response Bias**: Individuals choose themselves by responding to an open broadcast or survey (attracts strong, extreme opinions).
- **Response Bias**: Inaccurate answers caused by leading question wording, interviewer appearance, or self-reporting embarrassment.

## Unit 4: Probability Rules & Foundations

Probability problems on the AP exam reward careful notation and Venn diagram / tree diagram sketching.

### 1. Fundamental Rules
- **Complement Rule**: `P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)`
- **General Addition Rule (Any two events)**: `P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A and B)`
- **Mutually Exclusive (Disjoint) Events**: Events that cannot happen at the same time (`P(A and B) = 0`):
  `P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B)`
  *(Important: If two events with positive probabilities are mutually exclusive, they CANNOT be independent!)*

### 2. Conditional Probability & Independence
- **Conditional Probability Formula**: `P(A | B) = P(A and B) / P(B)`
- **General Multiplication Rule**: `P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B | A) = P(B) × P(A | B)`
- **Testing for Statistical Independence**: Events A and B are independent if and only if:
  `P(A | B) = P(A)` or `P(B | A) = P(B)` or `P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B)`

## Unit 5: Random Variables & Probability Distributions

Unit 5 bridges theoretical probability with sample distributions through discrete random variables, binomial models, and geometric models.

### 1. Discrete Random Variables
- **Mean (Expected Value)**: `mu_X = E(X) = sum[x_i × P(x_i)]`
- **Variance & Standard Deviation**:
  `Var(X) = sigma_X² = sum[(x_i - mu_X)² × P(x_i)]`
  `sigma_X = sqrt(Var(X))`

### 2. Linear Transformations & Combinations of Random Variables
If `Y = a + bX`:
- `mu_Y = a + b × mu_X`
- `sigma_Y = |b| × sigma_X`
- `sigma_Y² = b² × sigma_X²`

If combining two independent random variables (`X + Y` or `X - Y`):
- `mu_(X + Y) = mu_X + mu_Y` and `mu_(X - Y) = mu_X - mu_Y`
- `sigma_(X + Y)² = sigma_X² + sigma_Y²`
- `sigma_(X - Y)² = sigma_X² + sigma_Y²` *(variances ALWAYS add, even when subtracting random variables!)*
- `sigma_(X ± Y) = sqrt(sigma_X² + sigma_Y²)`

### 3. Binomial Distribution: `B(n, p)`
Requires the **BINS** conditions:
- **B** — Binary outcomes (Success or Failure).
- **I** — Independent trials.
- **N** — Number of trials `n` is fixed in advance.
- **S** — Same probability of success `p` on each trial.

**Formulas**:
- `P(X = k) = (n choose k) × p^k × (1 - p)^(n - k)`
- `mu_X = n × p`
- `sigma_X = sqrt(n × p × (1 - p))`

### 4. Geometric Distribution: `Geom(p)`
Counts the number of trials `Y` until the **first success** occurs:
- `P(Y = k) = (1 - p)^(k - 1) × p`
- `mu_Y = 1 / p`
- `sigma_Y = sqrt(1 - p) / p`

## Unit 6: Sampling Distributions & Central Limit Theorem

Understanding the difference between a population distribution, a sample distribution, and a **sampling distribution** is the central conceptual bridge in statistics.

### 1. Sampling Distribution of a Sample Proportion (`p_hat`)
- **Mean**: `mu_p_hat = p` (an unbiased estimator of the population proportion).
- **Standard Deviation**:
  `sigma_p_hat = sqrt((p × (1 - p)) / n)`
  *(Requires the 10% condition: `n < 0.10N` so trials are effectively independent).*
- **Normality Condition (Large Counts)**: If `n × p ≥ 10` and `n × (1 - p) ≥ 10`, the sampling distribution of `p_hat` is approximately normal.

### 2. Sampling Distribution of a Sample Mean (`x_bar`)
- **Mean**: `mu_x_bar = mu` (an unbiased estimator of the population mean).
- **Standard Deviation**:
  `sigma_x_bar = sigma / sqrt(n)`
  *(Requires the 10% condition: `n < 0.10N`).*

### 3. The Central Limit Theorem (CLT)
> **The CLT states**: As sample size `n` increases, the sampling distribution of the sample mean (`x_bar`) approaches a normal distribution, **regardless of the shape of the underlying population distribution**, provided `n ≥ 30`.

*(Common AP Mistake: The CLT does NOT say the population becomes normal, nor does it say the sample data becomes normal. Only the distribution of ALL POSSIBLE sample means becomes normal!)*

## Unit 7: Inference for Categorical Data (Proportions)

Inference involves two complementary procedures: estimating parameters using **confidence intervals** and evaluating claims using **significance tests**.

### 1. General Structure of a Confidence Interval
`Confidence Interval = Point Estimate ± (Critical Value) × (Standard Error)`

### 2. One-Sample `z`-Interval for a Proportion (`p`)
`CI = p_hat ± z* × sqrt((p_hat × (1 - p_hat)) / n)`
*(Common critical values: 90% confidence → `z* = 1.645`; 95% confidence → `z* = 1.960`; 99% confidence → `z* = 2.576`).*

### 3. One-Sample `z`-Test for a Proportion
`z = (p_hat - p0) / sqrt((p0 × (1 - p0)) / n)`
*(Notice: The denominator uses the hypothesized proportion `p0`, NOT `p_hat`, because we assume H0 is true).*

### 4. Two-Sample `z`-Test for Difference in Proportions (`p1 - p2`)
- **Pooled (Combined) Proportion**:
  `p_c = (x1 + x2) / (n1 + n2)`
- **Test Statistic**:
  `z = ((p_hat1 - p_hat2) - 0) / sqrt(p_c × (1 - p_c) × (1/n1 + 1/n2))`

### Mandatory Inference Conditions for Proportions:
1. **Random**: Data collected via random sampling or random assignment.
2. **10% Condition**: `n < 0.10N` (only required when sampling without replacement).
3. **Large Counts Condition**:
   - For intervals: `n × p_hat ≥ 10` and `n × (1 - p_hat) ≥ 10`.
   - For 1-sample tests: `n × p0 ≥ 10` and `n × (1 - p0) ≥ 10`.
   - For 2-sample tests: Verify for both samples!

## Unit 8: Inference for Quantitative Data (Means)

When analyzing numerical averages, we almost never know the true population standard deviation `sigma`. Consequently, we replace `sigma` with the sample standard deviation `s` and transition from the normal `z`-distribution to Student's `t`-distribution.

### 1. When to Use `t` vs. `z`
- Use `z` for **proportions** (`z`-tests / `z`-intervals).
- Use `t` for **means** whenever the population standard deviation `sigma` is unknown (which is 99% of all real-world inference problems).
- Student's `t`-distribution is symmetric and bell-shaped with heavier tails than the normal curve. As degrees of freedom (`df = n - 1`) increase, `t` converges toward `z`.

### 2. One-Sample `t`-Interval for a Mean (`mu`)
`CI = x_bar ± t* × (s / sqrt(n)),  df = n - 1`

### 3. One-Sample `t`-Test for a Mean
`t = (x_bar - mu0) / (s / sqrt(n)),  df = n - 1`

### 4. Paired `t`-Procedures (Matched Pairs)
- When data consists of paired observations from the same subject (e.g., Before vs. After, Left vs. Right), compute the single column of differences: `d_i = x1_i - x2_i`.
- Treat the differences as a **single sample**:
  `t = (x_bar_d - 0) / (s_d / sqrt(n)),  df = n - 1`

### 5. Two-Sample `t`-Procedures (Independent Groups)
`t = ((x_bar1 - x_bar2) - 0) / sqrt((s1² / n1) + (s2² / n2))`
*(Degrees of freedom: Use calculator software for Welch-Satterthwaite df, or use conservative `df = min(n1 - 1, n2 - 1)` by hand).*

### Mandatory Inference Conditions for Means:
1. **Random**: Random sample or randomized experiment.
2. **10% Condition**: `n < 0.10N` when sampling without replacement.
3. **Normal / Large Sample Condition**:
   - Population distribution is stated to be normal, OR
   - Sample size is large (`n ≥ 30`) by the Central Limit Theorem, OR
   - For small samples (`n < 30`), a graph of the sample data (dot plot, box plot, or stemplot) shows **no strong skewness and no outliers**.

## Unit 9: Chi-Square & Regression Slope Inference

Unit 9 encompasses testing distributions of categorical counts and running inference on least-squares regression lines.

### 1. The Chi-Square (`chi²`) Test Statistic
`chi² = sum[ (Observed - Expected)² / Expected ]`

### 2. The Three Chi-Square Tests
1. **Goodness of Fit Test**: Tests whether a single categorical variable matches a hypothesized theoretical distribution across `k` categories (`df = k - 1`).
2. **Test for Homogeneity**: Tests whether the distribution of a categorical variable is identical across two or more independent populations or treatment groups (`df = (rows - 1) × (cols - 1)`).
3. **Test for Independence**: Tests whether two categorical variables measured on a single population are associated or independent (`df = (rows - 1) × (cols - 1)`).

**Expected Count Formula for Two-Way Tables**:
`Expected = (Row Total × Column Total) / Table Grand Total`

*(Condition: All individual expected counts must be at least 5).*

### 3. Inference for Regression Slope (`beta`)
Tests whether there is a statistically significant linear relationship between two quantitative variables (`H0: beta = 0` vs. `Ha: beta ≠ 0`):
- `t = (b - 0) / SE_b,  df = n - 2`
- `Confidence Interval = b ± t* × SE_b`

**Conditions for Regression Inference (LINER)**:
- **L — Linear**: Scatterplot shows a linear pattern; residual plot shows random scatter.
- **I — Independent**: Observations are independent (10% rule if sampling).
- **N — Normal Residuals**: Histogram or dot plot of residuals shows no severe skewness or outliers.
- **E — Equal Variance**: Residual plot shows a roughly constant vertical band of spread.
- **R — Random**: Data gathered through random sampling or randomized assignment.

## Top AP Statistics Exam Tips (Guaranteed Score Boosters)

1. **Always State Your Context**:
   Never write "the mean is 12" or "there is a strong positive correlation." Write: "The mean *daily commute time* for teachers is 12 *minutes*," or "There is a strong, positive, linear relationship between *engine horsepower* and *highway fuel consumption*." Graders will dock points for naked numbers without context.
2. **Hypotheses MUST Use Population Parameters**:
   Always write `H0: mu = 50` or `H0: p = 0.40`. **Never use sample statistics** like `H0: x_bar = 50` or `H0: p_hat = 0.40`. We already know the sample values—hypotheses are tests about the unknown population!
3. **Show Your Condition Calculations Explicitly**:
   Do not just write "Large counts condition passes." Write the math: `np0 = 50 × 0.3 = 15 ≥ 10` and `n(1 - p0) = 50 × 0.7 = 35 ≥ 10`, so the sampling distribution is approximately normal.
4. **Interpret Confidence Intervals Correctly**:
   Use the standard template: "We are [95%] confident that the true [parameter in context] lies between [lower bound] and [upper bound]." Never say "there is a 95% probability that the parameter is inside the interval"—the true parameter is fixed, not random!
5. **Interpret P-Values Correctly**:
   "Assuming the null hypothesis is true, there is a [p-value] probability of obtaining a sample statistic as extreme as or more extreme than the observed statistic purely by chance."

## Frequently Asked Questions

### 1. Does the College Board give you a formula sheet on the AP Statistics exam?
Yes. Every student receives the official College Board AP Statistics Formula Sheet and Probability Tables during both Section I (Multiple Choice) and Section II (Free Response). However, the official sheet does NOT include condition checks (like the 10% rule or Large Counts), residual interpretations, or SOCS templates. You must memorize these concepts or use this cheat sheet to internalize them before exam day.

### 2. How do you describe a quantitative distribution on the AP exam?
Whenever an AP question asks you to describe or compare distributions, you must address **SOCS**: **Shape** (skewed right/left, symmetric, bimodal), **Outliers** (flag extreme values), **Center** (report median for skewed data, mean for symmetric data), and **Spread** (report IQR for skewed data, standard deviation for symmetric data). You must include the context of the data (units and variable name) to receive full credit.

### 3. What is the 1.5×IQR rule and do I need to memorize it?
The 1.5×IQR rule is the official College Board standard for identifying outliers. An observation is an outlier if it falls below the lower fence (`Q1 - 1.5 × IQR`) or above the upper fence (`Q3 + 1.5 × IQR`), where `IQR = Q3 - Q1`. This formula is NOT provided on the official College Board reference sheet, so you must memorize it.

### 4. When should you use a t-test versus a z-test?
Use **z-tests and z-intervals** for categorical **proportions** (`p` or `p1 - p2`). Use **t-tests and t-intervals** for quantitative **means** (`mu` or `mu1 - mu2`) whenever the population standard deviation (`sigma`) is unknown. In practice, `sigma` is almost never known in real-world studies, making the `t`-procedure the standard for means.

### 5. What conditions must be checked before running statistical inference?
For almost all AP inference procedures, you must verify three fundamental conditions:
1. **Random**: Data produced through a random sample or randomized experiment.
2. **10% Condition**: Sample size `n < 0.10N` when sampling without replacement to ensure independence.
3. **Normality / Sample Size**: For proportions, check the Large Counts condition (`n × p ≥ 10` and `n × (1 - p) ≥ 10`). For means, verify that `n ≥ 30` (Central Limit Theorem) or that the sample data displays no strong skewness or outliers.

### 6. How is the AP Statistics exam scored?
The AP Statistics exam consists of two 90-minute sections:
- **Section I (50% of composite score)**: 40 multiple-choice questions.
- **Section II (50% of composite score)**: 6 free-response questions (5 short FRQs and 1 Investigative Task).
Raw scores are combined into a composite score out of 100 and converted into the final AP grade (1 to 5). Historically, scoring approximately 70% or higher earns a score of 5.
